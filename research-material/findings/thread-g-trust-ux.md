# Thread G — Trust & UX: automation tolerance, local-only face recognition, video-editing scope, trust patterns

Research lane output. Compiled from web sources only; the four research questions are about people and are, by nature, mostly open. Sources are named inline (domain + page). Anything not directly supported by a retrieved source is marked **UNVERIFIED**.

---

## 1. How much automatic filing/organization do people tolerate before they feel out of control?

**No published threshold exists.** The literature gives design principles and mechanisms, not numbers. What it does give is consistent:

**Trust-in-automation research (academic).** Trust in automation is treated as the central variable governing reliance: too much trust produces overuse, too little produces disuse, and the goal is *calibrated* trust rather than maximal trust (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, "From Trust in Automation to Decision Neuroscience"; scispace.com review "Trust in Automation: Designing for Appropriate Reliance"). A 2024 review of trust-in-automation measurement confirms the field has many validated scales for trust levels but no prescriptive "tolerance threshold," because appropriate reliance depends on task risk and automation reliability in context (frontiersin.org, "Measurement of Trust in Automation"; pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, "Calibrating workers' trust in intelligent automated systems"). A separate line of that literature emphasizes that opaque communication and lack of shared ownership foster resistance or complacent overreliance (researchgate.net, "The Complexities of Human Factors in Automation Trust") — i.e., *how* the automation communicates matters as much as how often it's right.

**UX heuristics (NN/g).** Nielsen Norman Group's first heuristic, visibility of system status, explicitly links communicating current state to feeling in control and to trust (nngroup.com, "Visibility of System Status"). Their user-control heuristic (#3) frames easy undo and clear exits as what keeps users from feeling "at the mercy of your design" (nngroup.com, "User Control and Freedom"). Their newer AI-explainability work argues explanations tailored to the user's role are how trust is built, and that interactive AI assistants that "require user input, preferences, or approval throughout the process" sit between full autonomy and human workflows (nngroup.com, "Crafting AI Explanations"; "How Service Design Will Evolve with AI Agents").

**Email rules / inbox automation (closest analog to auto-filing).** The one rigorous need-finding study found (MIT CSAIL, Park et al., "Opportunities for Automating Email Processing," people.csail.mit.edu/soya/papers/mailbot.pdf, and the companion CHI'19 "Authoring Email Automation Rules" paper) that users want automation for *specific, repetitive, low-stakes* handling categories, and want to author and inspect the rules themselves. Practitioner write-ups converge on control-before-activation patterns: paused filters, a "shadow"/review-only mode where the rule only observes, an enforce mode applied only after inspection, and a decision history explaining why each message matched (keepknown.com, "What Is Inbox Management"), plus hygiene advice like test-then-enact, prune regularly, and never over-filter because "too many rules mean emails scattered everywhere" (sparkmailapp.com glossary). A practitioner taxonomy of consequence: classify/draft can be automatic; tagging a ticket "usually low consequence"; sending a customer-facing reply may need approval (aicompetence.org n8n write-up) — i.e., tolerance tracks the *undoability* of the automated action.

**DEVONthink (auto-classification in a notes/PIM context).** DEVONthink's design is itself evidence of a tolerance philosophy: the AI normally *suggests* (the See Also & Classify inspector shows candidate groups), while "Auto Classify" bulk-moves documents to the AI's best-fit group is the opt-in, heavier hammer (myproductivemac.com, "DEVONthink Part 5 - Classification"; devontechnologies.com help docs, "Inspectors: See Also & Classify"; discourse.devontechnologies.com, "DT3 auto group/auto classify"). Practitioner findings worth quoting-with-attribution: community testing of auto-tagging found roughly 50% of suggestions useful, but valued it anyway because it "removes the initial resistance created by an empty field" (discourse.devontechnologies.com, "Tags and AI or Machine Learning"). Suggestions degrade if the group structure is inconsistent, and improve as the database grows with topically consistent groups (discourse.devontechnologies.com, "Tips on Classify & See Also"; "Best Database Practices to auto-classify everything"). The global Inbox deliberately produces no classify suggestions until items are inside a database (discourse.devontechnologies.com, "Classify from the global Inbox") — automation is scoped away from the intake surface where users expect control.

**Merge/sync automation (Obsidian).** Obsidian Sync's default for markdown is automatic best-effort merging via diff-match-patch, which its own docs warn can produce duplicate text; the alternative is "create conflict file" so the user merges two versions manually, and per-device config is required (obsidian.md/help/sync/troubleshoot; deepwiki.com mirror of obsidian-help). Forum users explicitly ask for a "keep both versions, no silent merge" option out of fear of unnoticed data loss (forum.obsidian.md, "Option to let user manually resolve sync conflicts"); third-party post-mortems argue merging two finished files after the fact "is a guess" (baalda.com, "Why Obsidian sync conflicts happen"). Evidence that a nonzero population actively distrusts silent automatic merging even for plain text.

**Apple Photos as the high-tolerance end.** Apple auto-organizes via on-device ML (People & Pets, Memories, Featured Photos) with no filing action asked of the user (apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/photos; apple.com/privacy/features). People tolerate near-total automatic curation when the artifacts are ephemeral views (Memories) over an untouched original library — nothing is "filed away," everything is searchable anyway. This suggests the real variable may not be "how much automation" but "is the automatic organization destructive or merely additive."

**Synthesis (inference from the above, UNVERIFIED as a claim about users):** tolerance appears highest when (a) automation is additive over originals, not destructive; (b) it is visible (status, decision history); (c) it is reversible (undo, conflict files, suggestions-instead-of-moves); (d) failures are low-consequence (tags vs. sending). The specific tolerance ceiling for a notes app — auto-filing vs. auto-suggesting, and how many misfires before revolt — is not answered by any retrieved source.

## 2. Local-only face recognition: how on-device apps frame the risk surface

**Apple.** Apple's stated framing: "Face recognition and scene and object detection are done completely on your device rather than in the cloud. This allows Apple to provide you with these advanced features without accessing your photos" (apple.com/privacy/features, "Privacy - Features"). The Photos legal/privacy page adds that Apple "does not access your photos or videos, and does not use them for research and development," and that on-device ML powers People & Pets albums and Memories (apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/photos). Apple's ML research group has published the on-device person-recognition algorithm itself (machinelearning.apple.com, "Recognizing People in Photos Through Private On-Device Machine Learning") — publication of the method is part of the trust framing. The Platform Security Guide describes the Secure Enclave as the hardware root that "protects and evaluates the biometric data" for Face ID/Touch ID/Optic ID, with AES in-line file encryption elsewhere in the stack (help.apple.com, Apple Platform Security Guide). Note the boundary in Apple's framing: *photo-content* ML (faces, scenes) is on-device and keyed to "Apple doesn't access your photos"; Face ID biometric templates are separately secured in the Secure Enclave. **UNVERIFIED detail:** one low-quality source claims Photos stores 128-dimensional face embeddings locally; the retrieved Apple pages do not state embedding dimensions or where the Photos face embeddings are stored, so treat embedding specifics as unknown.

**Ente (E2EE photo storage with on-device ML).** Ente's framing is the most explicit about data flows, stated as a list of negatives: "All machine learning happens entirely on your device - your photos and ML data are never sent to Ente's servers" (ente.com, help: Machine Learning). The Security FAQ enumerates what servers never receive: unencrypted photos, face recognition data, search indexes, or "any information about what's in your photos"; photos are downloaded to the device, indexed locally, and the indexes are encrypted before being synced across the user's devices; no training on user data, by Ente or third parties (ente.com, help: Security and Privacy FAQ). The ML announcement post frames the contrast directly: zero knowledge of photos or search queries, derived data synced E2EE, queries processed locally (ente.com/blog, "Machine learning is live," 2024-11-30), and a follow-up argues the fundamental difference between server-side and on-device AI (ente.com/blog index). Operationally, they expose the split as two toggles: "Machine learning" enables the features, "Local processing" controls whether *this device* does the indexing or consumes indexes another of the user's devices built (ente.com, help: Search and Discovery FAQ). History note: the first desktop ML beta synced no indexes at all and would need re-indexing later — a deliberate early trade of convenience for locality (ente.com/blog, "Experimental on-device ML in Ente desktop," 2023-02-11).

**What stays local vs. what leaves (from these sources):** raw photos leave only inside E2EE ciphertext (Ente) or not at all (Apple, everything stays); derived data (face embeddings, groupings, search indexes) is computed locally; in the E2EE model it *syncs* but encrypted, so the server stores blobs it cannot read (Ente). Face-grouping works offline after initial indexing (ente.com, help: Search and Discovery).

**What a compromised index could leak (analysis, marked because no retrieved source addresses it directly — UNVERIFIED):** an attacker with the local index (not the photos) gets the face embeddings and the who-appears-in-whose-photos grouping graph — a social/co-occurrence map plus biometric identifiers usable for re-matching against other embeddings from the same model family, but not the images themselves. In the synced-E2EE design, the equivalent server-side compromise yields only ciphertext, pushing the attack to the client device. Neither Apple's nor Ente's retrieved pages discuss index-theft scenarios; their framing is "the server never sees it," which sidesteps rather than answers the local-compromise question. **UNVERIFIED: whether embedding-vs-raw-photo asymmetry materially reduces harm, and whether any vendor documents index theft as a threat model.**

## 3. Levels of video-editing ambition beyond trim-and-caption, and what each costs in scope

Ordered ladder, evidenced from vendor feature pages (CapCut, Descript, LumaFusion) and third-party reviews:

**Level 0 — Trim/segment.** CapCut's "Auto Cut" trims, segments, and syncs raw footage to an audio track, script, or beat pattern (capcut.com, "What Is Auto Cut in CapCut?"; capcut.com/resource/capcut-auto-cut). Scope cost: minimal — timeline in, timeline out. Every tool at every level does this.

**Level 1 — Captions/subtitles.** Auto-caption generation with selectable styles is CapCut's signature feature (capcut.com/resource/capcut-auto-cut); third-party testing reports accuracy in the low-to-mid 90s percent on clear single-speaker audio, citing CapCut's own documentation (offshoreclipping.com review — practitioner estimate, UNVERIFIED as vendor-independent). Descript reaches the same feature through a transcription-first model (descript.com). Scope cost: speech-to-text plus alignment; per-word timing data becomes a core data model requirement.

**Level 2 — Speech cleanup / silence and filler removal.** Descript: remove filler words, remove retakes, shorten word gaps, Edit for Clarity; these exist both as manual AI-tool buttons and as Underlord instructions (help.descript.com, "Underlord (Beta)"; descript.com/underlord). Studio Sound regenerates audio to remove noise (descript.com/video-editing). Scope cost: transcript editing must stay synchronized with the timeline (deleting a word deletes footage), which is Descript's entire architecture ("edit your words to edit your video," descript.com).

**Level 3 — Agentic/light multi-edit.** Descript's Underlord executes a whole instruction — "remove all retakes and tighten the pacing" — identifying bad takes, deleting them, shortening silences (chasejarvis.com analysis of Underlord); it does clip selection, scene generation, social captions, multicam edits from a chat prompt (descript.com/blog Underlord primer; prizmad.com review). Scope cost: a reviewable change model — Descript stresses you can "review or undo every change" (descript.com/features) — plus AI credits as a metered cost (descript.com/pricing, 400 AI credits/month on Hobbyist).

**Level 4 — Multi-track editing.** CapCut: multi-track timeline layering video, images, stickers, text, effects, picture-in-picture (capcut.com/tools/auto-video-editor; capcut.com/resource/pc-professional-video-editor), keyframe animation on all settings, optical-flow slow motion, speed curves, chroma key (play.google.com CapCut listing). LumaFusion is the reference point for "pro" multitrack on mobile: originally six video-with-audio plus six audio-only tracks, magnetic *and* track-based timelines, markers, voiceover, adjustable track heights (apkpure.net LumaFusion listing; luma-touch.com/timeline); v5.2 doubled it to 12 video and 12 audio tracks and added adjustment clips (redsharknews.com; provideocoalition.com, both Feb 2025). Scope cost: a real timeline engine (track management, magnetic vs. free positioning, per-track mute/lock/mix), keyframe system, and much heavier device-performance engineering; LumaFusion ships a PDF reference guide of that magnitude (luma-touch.com LumaFusion Reference Guide).

**Level 5 — Compositing/effects.** CapCut: green-screen/chroma keying, animated overlays, AI element alignment, layered blending (capcut.com/tools/auto-video-editor; creatorstackclub.com review adds opacity and blending modes). LumaFusion: layered effects with chroma and luma keys, unlimited keyframes, LUTs, scopes, multilayer titles, parametric EQ and audio ducking (apkpure.net listing). Descript crosses into this territory from the opposite direction: background replacement ("Green Screen"), AI eye-contact correction, voice cloning/regeneration (descript.com/video-editing; descript.com/pricing). Scope cost: a rendering/compositing pipeline (keying, blending, per-layer effects) plus, for the AI-assisted variants, generative models — the step where scope, compute, and QA cost all jump sharply. **UNVERIFIED (my synthesis, not from sources):** the cost curve is superlinear between levels 4 and 5, and generative features (Descript's Eye Contact/Studio Sound, CapCut's AI generation) carry ongoing per-use compute costs that one-time-purchase apps like LumaFusion avoid by offering none — which is itself a scope decision.

**Reading across the three vendors:** CapCut spans levels 0–5 on one product with a free tier and a Pro tier gating convenience (watermark-free templates, priority processing) rather than capability (ruahcreativehouse.org version-comparison table, third-party, UNVERIFIED in details); Descript spans 0–3 plus selective 5 via generative AI, organized around text; LumaFusion spans 0, 4, 5 with no automation at all — the "ambition" axis is orthogonal to the "automation" axis.

## 4. Trust patterns for local-first / zero-knowledge apps

**Pattern A — plain-language negative claims (what never happens).** Signal: "Signal cannot sell, rent, or monetize your data or content in any way – ever… designed to never collect or store any sensitive information," with messages unreadable by Signal because they are always E2EE (support.signal.org, Signal & GDPR). Ente enumerates exactly what the server never receives (see §2). Apple: "Apple does not access your photos or videos, and does not use them for research and development" (apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/photos). The strongest versions of this pattern are *specific* (a list of things that don't leave) rather than *general* ("we respect your privacy").

**Pattern B — verifiable proof (open source + independent audits).** Proton states the mechanism outright: "trust is earned through transparency and peer review. All our apps are open source and independently audited" (proton.me homepage; proton.me/community/open-source: "Anyone can inspect our software and confirm our encryption works"). They publish audit reports publicly per product (proton.me/blog/security-audit for Proton Mail; Cure53 audit of Proton Pass, proton.me/blog/pass-open-source-security-audit) and add operational attestation (SOC 2 Type II, proton.me/blog/soc-2) and aggregate transparency reports on legal orders (proton.me/legal/transparency). Signal's open-source clients let researchers verify the encryption works as claimed (reddit r/signal discussion summarizing the position; blakecrosley.com: "Closed-source security is fundamentally 'trust us'; open-source security is 'verify us'"). Third-party reviews function as distributed proof: Mozilla's independent privacy review of Signal ("can be used out-of-the-box with almost no impact on your privacy") and PCMag's review both feed trust; PCMag also documents Signal's 2022 third-party breach disclosure as trust-building done right (mozillafoundation.org; pcmag.com).

**Pattern C — product-level receipts (the feature demonstrates the claim).** Ente makes the E2EE-ML claim *visible in the product* via the Machine learning / Local processing toggles and by stating searches run locally (ente.com help pages). Apple pairs the on-device claim with permission gates ("apps can access your photos only with your permission," apple.com/privacy/features). Ink & Switch's local-first essay supplies the underlying principle: local-first apps "can use end-to-end encryption so that any servers that store a copy of your files only hold encrypted data that they cannot read," and it explicitly advises practitioners to "make clear to users when the data is stored only on their device versus being transmitted to a backend" (inkandswitch.com, "Local-first software"). The same essay frames trust as ownership: cloud apps put data on "someone else's computer," and local-first restores user agency — ownership in the sense of control, not IP (inkandswitch.com). Notably, the essay treats communicating sync/version state in decentralized systems as an *open research question* for HCI (inkandswitch.com), i.e., even the best local-first thinkers do not claim to have solved trust communication for their architecture.

**What these patterns share:** none of the successful examples rest on a slogan. Claim + verifiability + in-product demonstration is the recurring triple. **UNVERIFIED (no retrieved source measures this):** which pattern actually moves *users'* belief — audit publication, negative-claims pages, and in-product indicators are asserted by the vendors as trust mechanisms, and their effectiveness with ordinary (non-technical) users has not been established by any study retrieved here. It is also unverified whether audit-based trust (Proton/Signal) transfers to a small notes app that cannot yet afford recurring audits; the fallback in that case is full open source plus narrow, checkable negative claims.

## Cannot be settled without user testing

- The actual tolerance threshold for auto-filing in a notes app: how many automatic actions, and what misfire rate, before users feel out of control. The literature provides the variables (visibility, reversibility, consequence) but no numbers.
- Whether users want silent auto-merge (Obsidian default) or conflict files (Obsidian opt-in) for notes content — the forum demand and the shipped default disagree, and only testing with real users' notes settles it.
- Whether suggestion-style filing (DEVONthink Classify inspector) versus applied filing (Auto Classify) changes adoption in practice, and at what suggestion-accuracy floor users stop trusting the suggestions (~50% was partially tolerated in one practitioner test; where the floor sits is unknown).
- Which trust communication pattern (negative claims, audits, in-product indicators) actually increases belief for this app's audience; none of the retrieved sources contain user data on this.
- Whether "face data never leaves the device" registers with ordinary users at all, or whether they conflate it with cloud-photo-backup fears; also whether the embedding-theft risk (§2, UNVERIFIED) matters to users or only to security reviewers.
- Which video-editing ambition level our target users actually want and will pay for, and whether agentic editing (Underlord-style) with review/undo is trusted more or less than manual passes.

## Bottom line

The research questions are about people, and the existing record answers the *design* half well and the *measurement* half not at all. Known-good mechanics: automation that is additive rather than destructive, visible (status, decision history), reversible (undo, conflict files, review-every-change), and scoped by consequence (tag yes, send no). Known-good trust communication: specific negative claims ("X never leaves your device"), backed by open source and published audits, demonstrated in-product. What is genuinely unknown, and needs user testing: the tolerance ceiling itself, the silent-merge vs. conflict-file preference, the suggestion-accuracy floor, and which trust pattern this audience actually believes.

## Open sub-questions

- Does the Ink & Switch CRDT finding that "users surprisingly rarely encounter conflicts" hold for a notes app where edits are small and asynchronous, versus their co-present Kanban/drawing prototypes? (inkandswitch.com)
- Is the Apple-style "everything is a view over an untouched library" model (Memories, smart folders) the actual answer to the control question — i.e., is auto-*organization* only scary when it moves things?
- For E2EE-synced ML indexes (Ente model): what does the vendor document about client-side compromise, and is there any published threat model for stolen face embeddings? Not found in retrieved sources; worth a targeted follow-up.
- Do metered AI costs (Descript's AI credits) shape which ambition levels are economically viable for a local-first, self-hosted product that cannot rely on cloud compute?
- Is there any HCI study of the "review or undo every change" pattern for AI editing actions specifically, rather than general undo?
